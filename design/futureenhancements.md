# Future enhancements

A brainstorm, not a plan. These are one hundred directions someone could take Window Seat. They are grouped so we can tell a small improvement from a different product. Plenty of them are here because we might look at them and decide not to build them. Nothing on this list is scheduled. The site we have is one reading room, improved in small steps.

The shelf today is seven short public-domain reviews, eight blog posts, one poem, a search that looks through book reviews or blog posts (one at a time) by one genre, one author, one tag, and the text of a piece, a bio for Naomi Pell, hash addresses, and a floating menu. Suggestions below start from that room.

A few of them are written down so we can decide not to build them. They live in the categories with everything else, and they are listed together here:

- Accounts, likes, comments, follower counts, and personalized recommendations: 84, 85, 90, 100.
- A second layout, extra genre menus, two books on a blog post, audio, or a turn into a magazine: 30, 33, 38, 49, 50, 59, 60.
- Jokes and extras that would clutter the room: 73, 77, 96, 97.

## The reading page

1. A “read so far” marker that remembers where you stopped in a long review, and clears when you finish.
2. Estimated reading time, computed from the essay, shown beside the publication date.
3. A quieter related-posts list that offers three neighbors by shared tag, instead of the whole shelf.
4. Previous and next links at the bottom of a review, in shelf order.
5. A print stylesheet that drops the menu, the related list, and the covers, and keeps the essay and its metadata.
6. Footnote-style asides for a definition, so a term like “entailed” can be explained without a parenthesis in the paragraph.
7. A “copy a quote” control on pull quotes, with the book title and the site address attached.
8. A progress hairline at the top of a long essay.
9. The option to collapse the spoiler warning’s body by default is already here; a matching “skip spoilers” switch for the whole visit would hide every spoiler block.
10. Endnotes that collect every quotation in a review, for readers who want the evidence in one place.

## Notes and rereading

11. A downloadable blank reading-journal page, matching the fields in the journal post, as a printable sheet.
12. A “passages” section on a review: three short excerpts the essay actually discusses, each with a chapter hint.
13. A reread tag that is already possible, plus a small index of every post tagged `rereading`.
14. A private, on-this-device list of books you mean to reread. No account. It would live in the browser and could be cleared.
15. A “five lines” template in the journal post that can be copied as Markdown.
16. Dates for a first reading and a second reading, when a review is revised after a reread.
17. A margin-note style for the poem, so “Margin Light” can carry one gloss without becoming an essay.
18. A yearly rereading list, written as a post, not as a database.
19. A way to mark a review as “abandoned on purpose,” for books Naomi did not finish, which the bio already admits she keeps a list of.
20. Sample journal entries, fictional and short, so the journal post has a picture of the finished note.

## The review as a form

21. A shorter review length, on purpose, beside the long ones, so the shelf shows both.
22. A “who it is for” line, required, separate from the dek, since the writing guide already asks for it.
23. A reservation heading used consistently, so a reader can jump to the doubt.
24. Star-free ratings with words only: lend, keep, do not start. Easy to add, and easy to regret if it turns the essays into scores.
25. Original-language titles next to translations, for Homer and Cervantes.
26. Translator credit, the day a review is of a translation rather than a public-domain English text.
27. A content note that is not a spoiler: violence, grief, cruelty to animals. Separate from the spoiler flag.
28. A series title of its own, separate from the books a review can already list.
29. A “paired reading” between two reviews that argue with each other, such as the two Brontë novels.
30. Audio of Naomi reading the first paragraph. Appealing, and a real production burden. We might leave it.

## Search, tags, and the catalog

31. Remember the last search in the address, so a link can point at “Gothic novel or Gothic romance, tagged letters.”
32. Search the poem, once it has tags of its own.
33. A third genre menu. Probably refuse it. Two was the brief.
34. Synonyms, so `horror` also offers `gothic`, with the mapping written down where an editor can see it.
35. A tag index page, one page per tag, for people who do not want the search form.
36. Genre pages that read like a small introduction, not only a filtered list.
37. Show the book’s original year as its own labeled fact, beside the post’s publication date, on every card and not only inside the book line.
38. Let a blog post name two books, the way a review already can. The metadata would get harder. We may not want that.
39. A controlled vocabulary file, so tags cannot drift into `Gothic` and `gothic` and `the gothic`.
40. Search inside the essay text is already on the search page, sorted by how often the words appear. A later pass could highlight the matches in the piece itself.

## Beside the reviews

41. One more poem, if it earns a place, rather than a poem section that expects a stream.
42. A letters section: Naomi writes to a specific reader about a book, shorter than a review.
43. A “left unfinished” series, honest and short.
44. Posts that annotate a single paragraph, using the three-pass method on a passage we have permission to quote.
45. A commonplace book page: sentences collected from the reviews, each linking back.
46. Seasonal shelves, assembled by hand: winter ghosts, summer roads. Editorial, not algorithmic.
47. A glossary of the few technical words the tool posts have to use, written once and linked.
48. An “on the desk” post that is only what Naomi is reading now, dated, replaced when it changes.
49. Interviews. We do not know any living authors through this project yet, and public-domain authors cannot answer. Easy to postpone forever.
50. Fiction of our own. This is a book blog. Original stories would change what the site is. Probably not.

## The look of Window Seat

51. Slightly wider measure on very large screens, tested against a long Melville paragraph.
52. A dark reading theme that keeps the clay feeling, for the evening shift Naomi used to work.
53. Cover drawings for the blog posts that currently have no jacket, simple and consistent with `BookCover`.
54. The home grid could show the poem as type rather than a fake jacket.
55. A smaller menu label set, if Search, Bio, and About start to feel like furniture.
56. The related-posts column could hide on the search page’s neighbors and stay only on essays. It already stays off the indexes.
57. Drop caps on the first paragraph, used once and then judged. Inkwell had them. Window Seat might not want them.
58. A favicon that is a window, not a generic mark, drawn in the same spirit as the jackets.
59. Seasonal palette shifts. Pretty, and a way to make the site feel like a theme club. We might skip it.
60. Bring back a second layout beside Window Seat for comparison. This is the decision we already closed. Leave it closed unless a real reading problem shows up.

## Comfort and access

61. A type-size control with three steps, remembered on this device.
62. A “more space between lines” control for the essay only.
63. Respect a system setting for reduced transparency on the cover-tile gradients.
64. Check the clay-on-cream contrast of muted metadata against WCAG, and darken `--muted` if it fails.
65. The tag cloud on search should remain usable with a keyboard. It is buttons now; keep it that way if the cloud grows.
66. A skip link already exists. Add a second one, “skip to search,” only on the search page, if the cloud gets long.
67. Transcripts, if we ever add audio. No audio yet, so this is a condition, not a task.
68. Avoid moving covers or tilting cards. The old scatter layout did that. Window Seat should stay still.
69. Test the essay, the search form, and the bio at a phone width and at a large desktop width whenever the layout changes.
70. Open Library covers and the drawn jackets already name themselves for a screen reader (`Cover of …`). Write alt text the same way for any future image.

## The librarian’s desk

71. A “ask for a book” note that tells a reader what to say in an email: last book finished, mood wanted, mood refused.
72. A holding-shelf metaphor for a short list Naomi would pull for a patron, written as a post with a date.
73. Dewey numbers as a joke in the metadata. Funny once. Clutter after that. Probably not.
74. A page of reference questions she liked, with the book that answered them.
75. Interlibrary-loan realism: a note in the finding-books territory about how long a hold takes, for readers who do not buy books.
76. A “library copy” flag on a review, meaning she read a borrowed book and the pages had someone else’s pencil.
77. Hours and a map for the fictional Millrace library. It is a persona, not a building. Do not invent an address people might try to visit.
78. A staff-picks voice that stays first person, so it does not pretend to be an institution.
79. Readers’ advisory formulas (“if you liked the letter scene, try this”) generated by hand from tags, a dozen at a time, not by a model.
80. A correction policy: how a reader reports a wrong date or a mistyped name, and where we say so on the bio page.

## Readers, clubs, and a following

81. A monthly letter, linked from the blogging post, with one new essay and one old one.
82. A book-club printable: the questions from the club post, plus blank lines, for one specific novel.
83. An email address or a form that does not require an account. A mailto link is enough until the volume is real.
84. Comments. They need moderation, spam handling, and a place to put them on a static site. Easy to want, easy to regret. Default is no.
85. Likes and claps. They would change the tone of the room. Probably not.
86. A “discuss with a friend” link that shares a single pull quote rather than the whole essay.
87. Guest posts from other librarians. Only if the voice stays as plain as the current posts.
88. A public reading list for a real book club, updated by editing a Markdown file, not by a dashboard.
89. Social cards for each essay, generated, so a shared link shows the book and not a blank rectangle.
90. Follower counts, analytics dashboards, and growth charts on the site itself. The blogging post argues against this. Do not put them on the page.

## Finding what to read next

91. A “start here” path of four reviews for someone new to the nineteenth century.
92. “If you only have an evening” picks, restricted to the shorter posts and the poem.
93. Content warnings and “gentle” versus “severe” as tags, used sparingly.
94. A map of settings: Yorkshire, the sea, the city, the drawing room. Fun, and a lot of research to do honestly.
95. Readalikes that point off the shelf to books still in copyright, with no pretend review attached.
96. Age guidance. The books are adult classics. A single sentence on the about page may be enough. A rating system is probably not.
97. “Pairs with” food or music. Charming in a bookshop, slightly cute here. We might skip it.
98. Seasonal challenge posts (“three ghost stories”) that use the shelf instead of assigning homework.
99. A page that admits which famous books are not here yet, so the shelf does not look like a canon.
100. Personalized recommendations from an account profile. That is a different product, and it needs accounts we have deliberately not built. Leave it out.
