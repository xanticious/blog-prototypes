Book clubs die of two causes: nobody finished the book, or one person talks as if the living room were a *panel*. Both are solvable. You do not need a syllabus, a themed snack, or a theory of the novel. You need a shared stretch of pages, a start time, and questions that more than one kind of reader can answer.

## Before the meeting

Choose the next book together, and say out loud how many pages a week that implies. A difficult book is a fine choice if the group has agreed to a slower pace. Surprise difficulty is how people stay home.

Agree on spoilers. A simple rule works: discuss the whole book, and anyone halfway through can ask to pause a thread. Do not spend the evening hinting. Hints are more annoying than endings.

If you are hosting, prepare six questions and expect to use three. Write them down. Memory is a bad moderator. Good questions point at a choice inside the book, not at a referendum on whether it was “good.” Compare these:

- “Did you like the ending?”
- “What does the ending ask us to believe about forgiveness, and did you believe it?”

The second question still lets people dislike the book. It just gives them something to point at.

## Questions that make room

Keep a mix:

- **A concrete question.** Which scene stayed with you on the walk to the car.
- **A character question.** Who did you trust, and when did you stop.
- **A making question.** What is the book doing with its narrator, its order of events, its title.
- **A life question, used sparingly.** Did this remind you of a real argument, job, or family rule. These can take over. They are valuable when they return to the page.
- **A dissent question.** What did the book get away with. Where were you unconvinced.

Read one short passage aloud. A paragraph is enough. Shared attention repairs a group that has been trading opinions without evidence. People who “are not good at themes” are often excellent at noticing a weird detail in a paragraph someone just read. That detail is an entrance.

## While you are in the room

Ask the quiet person about the passage, not about their feelings in general. “What did you make of the mother’s silence there” is easier to answer than “Any thoughts?”

If someone summarizes the plot for twenty minutes, thank them and turn to a question that assumes the summary is done. If someone is wrong about a fact, correct the fact gently and move. Most heated club arguments are about two different memories of chapter twelve.

You do not have to reach a verdict. A meeting that ends with “we saw that scene differently” has succeeded. Write down the next book before people stand up. Future-you, tired and standing by the door, will not invent a fair process.

## If you are starting alone

A club can be three people and a monthly call. It can also be one friend and a walk. The format in this guide is only a way of keeping the book in the center of the conversation, so the evening is about reading and not about who is best at speaking. The person who brings a marked page is doing more for the group than the person who brings a speech. *Dracula* is a generous choice for this kind of evening, because the book is already a stack of voices that do not agree.
