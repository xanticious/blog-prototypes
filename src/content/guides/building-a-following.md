*Pride and Prejudice* begins with a neighborhood that already knows how to talk. News moves by visit and by letter. Nobody builds an audience in a week, because an audience is just people who have come to expect you. A blog works the same way, with worse stationery.

You can start before anyone is watching. That is the best time to start. The early posts teach you what you sound like when you are not performing for a crowd that has not arrived.

## Start a shelf, not a brand

Choose one address and one promise. The promise can be small: “I write about the book I just finished, twice a month, for readers who like a scene more than a star rating.” Put that sentence where a new visitor can see it, in ordinary words.

Write the first four posts before you worry about a logo. A review, a note on how you take notes, a list of books you abandoned, a letter about a reread. Four pieces are enough for a stranger to know whether they want to come back. One piece is a flyer.

Pick a rhythm you can keep in a bad month. Once a month is a real blog. Daily is a job. The readers you want can tell the difference between a pace and a panic.

## Where people actually come from

They rarely come from the void. They come from a place where they were already talking about books.

- Tell the book club, and bring one printed page or one link, not a speech.
- Comment on other people’s reviews with a specific sentence about their point. “I also distrusted the narrator in the kitchen chapter” is a contribution. “Great post!” is a knock on an empty door.
- Use one social account the way you would use a noticeboard at a library: a line, a link, a question. You do not have to be on all of them.
- Keep an email list when you are ready. A short letter when a new essay is up will outlast a social post. Services such as Buttondown or Substack can send the letter. The blog can still be the home. The email is the bell.

Say who the writing is for, out loud, more than once. “People who read on the train and do not want homework” gives a stranger permission to stay. “Content for book lovers” gives them nothing to picture.

## What a following is

A following is not a number that proves you are a good reader. It is a few people who would notice if you stopped, because the next post was part of how they find books. Twenty of those people is a beginning. Two hundred is a room. You do not need the room to write the next true paragraph.

Answer the notes you receive. Remember what someone said they were reading. Recommend a book in the open, with the reason, and expect nothing back. That is the whole social skill.

If you want a practical first month: publish one review, one shorter note, tell five real people the address, and ask one of them what they are reading. Then do it again next month. The neighborhood does not assemble itself. It also does not require a trumpet.
