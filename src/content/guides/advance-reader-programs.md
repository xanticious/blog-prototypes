An advance reader copy is a book a publisher lets you read before the day it officially comes out, so a review, a bookseller’s note, or a librarian’s list can exist when everyone else is allowed to buy it. People shorten that to **ARC**. You will also see “advanced reader copy” and “advanced reading copy.” They mean the same early book. An “advance reader program” or “advanced reader program” is the publisher’s system for handing those copies out.

This is not the same thing as a school or library program for a young person who is ready for harder books. That one is about the reader’s pace. Ask a librarian. They already keep those lists, and they will not ask you for a follower count. This post is about the publisher kind, because that is the one new bloggers hear about and cannot find the door to.

*A Tale of Two Cities* is a reminder that books have always had a public life before the private one of reading them alone. Serialization, reviews, arguments in newspapers. An ARC is a modern, smaller version of “please read this while the ink is still settling,” with rules attached.

## What you are actually joining

You are not being hired, and you are not owed a free book. A publisher, or a service the publisher uses, is trading an early copy for the chance of a timely, honest response in a place other readers look. The places people start are usually:

- **NetGalley**, a website where you request digital copies and report back.
- **Edelweiss**, used heavily by booksellers and librarians, and open enough that a serious blogger can learn it.
- A publisher’s own form, often linked from their website or a newsletter for reviewers.
- A publicist’s email, which tends to arrive after you have already reviewed a few of their books in public.

Create one profile. Say who you are, what you read, and where the review will appear. Link the blog, even if it is new. If you also talk about books at a library, a bookshop, or a book club, say so. Specific is more convincing than “I love to read.”

## How to ask, the first time

Request one or two books you would read anyway. A niche helps: historical novels, horror, essays, picture books, poetry in translation. A profile that says yes to everything looks like a shelf with no taste.

Read the date. Many copies may be reviewed only after a certain day, or not quoted past a certain length. Put the date in your calendar when you download the file, not when you finish the book.

Write the review you would have written if you had paid for the copy. You do not owe praise. You do owe a timely post if you said you would post, and a short note on the request site if they ask whether you reviewed it. If you cannot finish, say so. Silence makes the next request harder.

Do not sell the file. Do not post the file. It is a loan with extra steps.

## If the answer is no

Early requests are often declined. A new blog has no record yet. That is ordinary. Review library books, used books, and the public-domain novels on a shelf like this one. Publish on a rhythm you can keep. Then ask again, still for books you actually want, and mention the reviews that already exist.

A book blog can be excellent and never join one of these programs. Advance copies are a convenience and a responsibility. They are not a license to have opinions. You already have those.
