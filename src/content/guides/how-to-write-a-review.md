A book review sounds, from the outside, like a performance of authority. The good ones sound, from the inside, like attention. You are telling a future reader what it feels like to live in this book, what the book seems to care about, and who might want to walk in after you. None of that requires a literature degree. It requires notes you took while you still remembered the exact scene.

## Start with three sentences you will not publish

Before you try to sound like an essay, write three plain sentences in a notebook or at the top of a file:

1. What happens that you still see, in one scene.
2. What the book seems to be arguing, or loving, or afraid of.
3. Who you would hand it to, and who you would warn.

These sentences are your private brief. They keep the draft from turning into a plot summary with a star rating taped to the end. You may throw them away later. Their job is to stop you from starting with “This book is about a man who…”

## Borrow a shape, then bend it

Most reviews that people finish reading have a shape something like this:

- An opening that starts inside the book, not above it. A scene, a sentence, a room.
- A little context: who wrote it, when, and what kind of book it is. Two or three sentences is enough.
- A closer look at one choice the writer made. Point of view, structure, a character who will not fit the theme, a chapter everyone wants to skip.
- A reservation, if you have one. Reviews that admit a rough patch are more trustworthy than reviews that levitate.
- A closing note about who it is for. “For readers who like quests” is useful. “A masterpiece for the ages” is fog.

You can change the order. You should not try to cover every character. A review is not a book report that proves you finished. It is a path through the book that a stranger can walk.

## Use small evidence

Quote briefly, and only when the sentence does something you cannot paraphrase. A line of dialogue, a description of a room, a narrator’s slip: one or two of these will do more than a page of general praise. If you are writing about a book that is not in the public domain, keep quotations short. Your words should be the essay. The author’s words are exhibits.

When you describe a plot, stay on the near side of the ending unless you have warned the reader. Spoilers are sometimes necessary when the meaning lives in the last chapter. Say so, and then do the work. Do not hint and then refuse. That is a trailer, not a review.

## Sound like a person in a room

Write the way you explain a book to a friend who reads, not the way you imagine a newspaper column has to sound. Specific is better than grand. “I trusted the narrator until the chapter in the kitchen” is a real sentence. “The author brilliantly explores the human condition” could be taped onto any novel printed this century.

Read the draft aloud once. Anywhere you get bored, the reader will too. Cut the sentence that restates the sentence before it. End when the thought is finished, not when you have reached a word count.

## Then stop polishing and publish a version

A review can be four hundred words or two thousand. Length is a choice about depth, not a measure of seriousness. The reviews on this demo site are long on purpose, so the prototypes have real paragraphs to shape. Yours can be shorter. The only failure is the draft that stays in the notebook because it does not sound official yet. Official is not the job. Clear attention is the job.
