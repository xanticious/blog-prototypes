A reading journal fails in two elegant ways. The first is the empty notebook with thick paper, purchased in a hopeful January, still empty in March because every sentence had to be worthy of the paper. The second is the log that records only titles and stars, which you never reread because it does not contain a single remembered room. You want something between those: light enough to fill in on a tired night, specific enough to be useful in a year.

## Pick a home that is already open

Use the notes app you already unlock, a paper notebook that lives by the chair where you read, or a folder of plain text files. The technology does not matter. The distance from “I just finished a chapter” to “I can type one line” matters a great deal. If capturing a thought requires a login, a template, and a choice of tags, you will do it for a week.

This website keeps its demo essays as Markdown files in a folder. That is a publishing choice, described in the getting-started guide. Your private journal can be messier than anything you post. Mess is a sign of use.

## The same five lines, every time

When you finish a reading session, fill in only these, and give yourself permission to skip any line that does not spark:

1. **Where I stopped.** Page, chapter, or a one-line place marker. “Just after the letter.”
2. **One concrete thing.** An object, a line, a gesture. Not a theme.
3. **A question.** Even a grumpy one. “Why is the friend telling this story?”
4. **A reaction in plain speech.** Bored, furious, delighted, suspicious. Name it.
5. **A next step.** Keep going, or start a review, or hand this book to a specific person.

Five lines take less time than deciding how you feel about the book in a general way. Over a month, the concrete things become a private index. When you sit down to write a review, you are not staring at a fog called “I liked it.” You are staring at a list of rooms you actually visited.

## Once a book is finished

Add a short afterword, separate from the daily notes. Date it. Say whether you would reread it, and under what circumstances. Mention a book it talked to, if one came to mind. Then stop. A finished-book entry that tries to be the definitive account will keep you from writing the next day’s five lines.

If you like ratings, use them, but make the scale describe use rather than greatness. “Would lend” and “would not finish again” are more informative than seven out of ten. Stars without a sentence are how you forget you ever had a mind while reading.

## Reread the journal, not only the books

Every so often, read a month of notes straight through. You will notice habits: the kinds of scenes you copy out, the characters you forgive too quickly, the subjects you keep circling. That pattern is more valuable than a yearly list of books completed. A reading life is not a score. It is a set of returns. The journal is how you make the returns visible to yourself, in ink or in a file, before you ever decide to make them public on a blog. A single scene is enough for one day’s note.
