Alice follows a rabbit because she is bored, not because she is chosen. That small detail is the tone of the whole book. Lewis Carroll, the pen name of the mathematician Charles Dodgson, sends a practical child into a country of arguments, and the comedy comes from her refusal to stop applying ordinary sense. When a cake says “EAT ME,” she checks the label. When she is shrinking and growing without permission, she worries about whether she is still herself, and whether her feet will still obey her if she sends them boots by post. Wonderland is strange. Alice’s mind is orderly. The friction is the book.

It is easy to file *Alice’s Adventures in Wonderland* as a children’s fantasy and stop. Children do love it, and the images are unforgettable: the pool of tears, the Cheshire Cat’s lingering grin, the tea party stuck at six o’clock, the queen with a temper and a flamingo. Under the images is a tour of how language misbehaves when people treat puns as doors. “Why is a raven like a writing-desk?” is famous because it is a riddle that was published, for a long time, without an answer. Carroll understood that some questions are machines for keeping a conversation spinning.

## Manners, trials, and tiny tyrannies

Almost everyone Alice meets is certain, and almost everyone is rude. The Caterpillar contradicts her. The Duchess moralizes at violent speed. The Mad Hatter and the March Hare offer wine when there is no wine. The Queen’s croquet game changes the equipment whenever the equipment might produce a fair result. Carroll, who lived among Victorian rules of schooling and etiquette, builds a world where rules exist mainly to display the ruler. Alice’s most heroic act is not slaying anything. It is saying, at the trial of the Knave of Hearts, that the court is nonsense, and waking up.

> “Who in the world am I? Ah, that’s the great puzzle.”

The trial chapter is worth reading twice. The evidence is a poem. The verdict is prepared before the charge is clear. It is funny, and it is also a clean picture of a proceeding that has already decided who is allowed to speak. Alice grows back toward her real size as she argues. The body, in this book, is part of the logic. Being small makes her timid in some rooms and bold in others. Being large lets her finally pick up the table of jurors.

## The feeling underneath the jokes

There is a thread of anxiety that the jokes do not cancel. Alice is afraid of disappearing, of saying the wrong poem, of being mistaken for a serpent, of not being believed by her own cat. Growing up is treated as a series of ill-fitting rooms. You do not need a lecture about childhood to feel it. Carroll’s mathematics shows up as play: recursion, inversion, the way a statement can be true and useless. His ear for English shows up as play of another kind. Poems are parodies of improving verses schoolchildren were expected to recite. Getting them wrong is a small liberation.

## How to enjoy it without hunting symbols

You can write a serious essay about Alice, and many people have. You can also read it aloud, which is how it began, as a story told on a boat to the real Alice Liddell. Three pleasures, if you do:

1. The puns that refuse to stay decorations.
2. The **rules that are enforced and unfair**, which is the book’s real nonsense.
3. The kindness of getting to go home.

If you annotate, annotate the conversations, not just the creatures. Notice who interrupts, who demands a recitation, who changes a definition halfway through a sentence. Nonsense, in Carroll’s hands, is not the absence of rules. It is what rules sound like when they are no longer required to be fair. Alice gets to go home. The last paragraph, gently, reminds us that the sister left on the bank will grow up, and that the dream will become a story she tells. The book knows it is a book. That wink is part of its kindness.
