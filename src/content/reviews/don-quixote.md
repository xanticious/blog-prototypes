Alonso Quixano owns too many books of chivalry and not enough moderation. He renames himself Don Quixote, appoints his old horse Rocinante, and rides out to revive a profession the countryside is not hiring. Miguel de Cervantes published the first part of *Don Quixote* in 1605, into a Europe that already knew the knightly romance was a little tired. The joke could have been a short one: man mistakes windmills for giants, gets knocked down, goes home. Cervantes wrote a long one, and the length is where the book becomes more than a satire. A person who misreads the world is still a person in it. Other people have to decide whether to mock him, use him, or go with him.

Sancho Panza goes with him, for a promised island and then for reasons the promise does not cover. Sancho is earthy, proverbial, hungry, and increasingly fond. The pair is one of the great inventions in fiction because neither voice cancels the other. Quixote lifts a barber’s basin into the helmet of Mambrino. Sancho would like to know if the helmet can still be used as a basin. The novel lives in that argument.

## Books inside the book

Cervantes builds a story that knows it is being read. In the second part, published ten years later, Quixote and Sancho have become famous because a book of their adventures already exists. People they meet have read it. Some want to steer the knight into new episodes for their own entertainment. The reader is implicated. If you have been laughing at a man who cannot tell a puppet show from a battle, what exactly are you doing in your chair?

The episode of the library burning, early on, is a literary joke and a little murder. The priest and the barber try to cure Quixote by destroying the books that made him, sparing a few they personally approve. Censorship appears here as friendship. It does not work. Quixote’s imagination is no longer a stack of paper. It is a way of walking.

> “Finally, from so little sleeping and so much reading, his brain dried up and he went completely out of his mind.”

## Cruelty, and the turn away from it

Some of the jokes are rough. Quixote is beaten. Sancho is tossed in a blanket. People in inns treat a delusion as a sport. Cervantes does not pretend this is always charming, and as the second part goes on he seems more protective of his pair. The long stretch in which Sancho actually governs an island — a fake island, arranged by a duke for amusement — lets a supposedly simple man judge cases with a practical mercy the court around him lacks. It is the novel’s quiet rebuttal. The squire has been listening the whole time. He has not become a knight. He has become harder to condescend to.

## Why it still feels new

*Don Quixote* is often called the first modern novel because it is so interested in how stories get into a head and then into a landscape. You can read it for the set pieces, and you should:

- The windmills, and the dignity that survives them.
- Sancho’s **fake island**, where a supposedly simple man judges with mercy.
- The conversations between battles, which are the novel’s modern weather.

Then keep an eye on those conversations, when two men ride slowly and talk about what they are doing as if talking might make a shape of it. Quixote’s deathbed renunciation is sober. Whether you take it as a cure or as a loss says a lot about what you think fiction is for. Cervantes, sly to the end, has already made the stronger case. He shows a reader so changed by books that the world has to reorganize itself around him — not always kindly, not always falsely, and never again as if stories were only ink.
