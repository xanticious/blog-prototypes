A tenant named Lockwood arrives at Wuthering Heights, mistakes his landlord for a rustic, and is pinned into a nightmare by a child’s name scratched on a windowsill: Catherine. He gets out as fast as the weather allows and asks the housekeeper, Nelly Dean, what on earth he has walked into. Emily Brontë then tells one of the strangest novels in English almost entirely through Nelly’s voice, with Lockwood as a polite, slightly foolish frame. We are not in the storm. We are in the kitchen, hearing about the storm from someone who survived it and has opinions.

That structure matters, because *Wuthering Heights* is constantly being misremembered as a simple romance between Catherine Earnshaw and Heathcliff. The passion is real. It is also tangled up with class humiliation, inheritance, sibling cruelty, and two houses that function like opposing arguments. Wuthering Heights is exposure. Thrushcross Grange is upholstery. People cross between them and are damaged by the crossing.

## Not a metaphor you can tidy

Heathcliff is brought home by old Mr. Earnshaw like a living disruption and is never allowed to forget the doorway he came through. Hindley degrades him. Catherine chooses Edgar Linton and delivers the line that has been printed on too many posters: “I am Heathcliff.” In context it is not a wedding vow. It is a confession that she has split her life into a socially possible self and a self she thinks of as more real, and that she expects the world to honor both. It does not.

Brontë refuses the comfort of a villain who is only a villain. Heathcliff’s revenge is methodical and often sickening. He targets the next generation, which is the detail that should stop anyone from calling this a beautiful love story and stopping there. Hareton, Cathy the younger, Linton Heathcliff: the book’s second half is about whether damage is hereditary law. Nelly, practical and sometimes self-justifying, keeps house through all of it. Trust her, and don’t trust her completely. She is a narrator with a job to keep.

> “Whatever our souls are made of, his and mine are the same.”

## The moor is not scenery

The weather in this novel behaves like a character that cannot be appealed to. Windows open onto violence. Graves soften only after the people are done using each other. Brontë grew up in a parsonage on the edge of the Yorkshire moors, and she writes land as something that does not care about drawing-room motives. When Lockwood, at the end, looks at three headstones and wonders how anyone could imagine unquiet sleepers under so quiet a sky, the novel lets you sit with the doubt. Are the ghosts gone, or is Lockwood the wrong instrument for detecting them?

## A way to begin

Read the first three chapters twice: once for the ghost story, once for the social comedy of a city man mishandling a country house. Then keep a simple map of the two houses and the two Catherines. The names are a deliberate thicket. The feeling underneath is not thick at all. It is painfully clear. People who are denied status will sometimes try to become fate itself. Brontë watches that attempt to the end, without background music, in a kitchen voice that has seen the bread burned and the children frightened and will tell you both.
