Call me impatient, a reader might think, opening *Moby-Dick* and finding etymology, extracts, a sermon, and a hundred pages before the Pequod even loses sight of shore. Herman Melville seems to agree that a whale is too large for a single kind of chapter. He changes the form whenever the subject changes. There is theater, encyclopedia, slapstick, natural history, and a revenge plot told by a sailor who is sometimes in the scene and sometimes only the voice left over.

Ishmael is the gift that makes the rest readable. He is curious, underemployed, and willing to share a bed with a stranger at the Spouter-Inn before he is willing to sign on with a captain he has not met. His friendship with Queequeg is the book’s moral counterweight: an intimacy that does not require sameness. Whenever Ahab’s hunt threatens to become the only story, Ishmael is still looking sideways, at the try-works, at the crew’s countries, at the way work is actually done.

## Ahab is not the ocean

Ahab is unforgettable because Melville gives him language big enough to scar. The white whale took his leg, and he promotes that injury into a war against the unseen. The quarterdeck scene, where he nails the gold coin to the mast and pulls the crew into his oath, is one of the great seductions in fiction. You feel how a private wound can become a collective fate when the man with the wound controls the ship.

Melville does not let the seduction go unargued. Starbuck, the first mate, keeps saying the obvious thing: a whale is a whale; the business of the ship is oil; a man’s family is waiting. Starbuck loses the argument and keeps his clarity, which is a tragic combination. The book is honest about how often good sense arrives without the power to turn the wheel.

> “All visible objects, man, are but as pasteboard masks.”

## The chapters people skip

The so-called whale chapters — cetology, the anatomy of the head, the rope, the measurement — are where some readers bail and where the novel becomes itself. Melville is mocking the urge to classify a wonder, and he is also doing the classification with real attention. Work is holy and bloody in these pages. Men stand inside a dead whale’s head. Lamps burn the animal they are reading by. If you treat these chapters as obstacles on the way to the chase, you miss Melville’s point: the chase is what happens when someone refuses the world’s detail and wants only a symbol.

## A way in

You do not have to read *Moby-Dick* at a sprint, and you do not have to pretend every digression delights you. Read Ishmael’s beginning until you trust him. Read “The Quarter-Deck” aloud if you can. Then let the ship’s labor be part of the plot, not a pause in it. The ending is swift because the middle was vast. A ship organized around one man’s metaphor runs out of world. The ocean, which never agreed to be a metaphor, remains.
