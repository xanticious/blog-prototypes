Jonathan Harker’s journal begins like a young solicitor’s travel notes: paprika chicken, a delayed train, a castle in the Carpathians, a client who does not want his picture taken. The professionalism is the horror’s door. Bram Stoker’s *Dracula* lets ordinary record-keeping stumble into a predator, then asks whether the tools of a modern city — shorthand, medical notes, phonograph cylinders, railway timetables — can be turned around and used as weapons.

The novel is epistolary. No single narrator owns it. Mina Murray transcribes and collates. Dr. Seward dictates case notes about a patient who eats flies and speaks in riddles. A newspaper reports a ship that arrives in Whitby with a dead captain lashed to the wheel. Lucy Westenra’s decline is observed by three men who love her and one old professor who finally has a name for what is happening. The form is the argument. Evil, in this book, spreads through private rooms, and the answer is a shared archive.

## The count is a plan, not a mood

Dracula is memorable as a figure in a doorway, but Stoker spends more time on logistics than on fangs. The count studies English so he can pass. He ships boxes of earth because his power is tied to soil. He buys houses in London under other names. He is an aristocrat using the empire’s own transport. The terror is not only that he is undead. It is that he is organized, and that the city is full of thresholds no one is watching.

Renfield, the patient in the asylum next door to one of those houses, is the novel’s grotesque mirror. He practices a small theology of eating life in order to gain life. Dr. Seward records him as a case. The reader slowly realizes the case is a commentary on the count, and on any system that consumes in order to continue.

> “I want you to believe… to believe in things that you cannot.”

## Mina at the center

Film versions often push Mina to the side. The novel does not. She is the person who makes the documents searchable. She thinks. She is also targeted, and the book is unsettling about the way the men around her move from partnership to protection to secrecy “for her own good.” Stoker is not a modern feminist novelist, and some of the book’s science is pure period panic. What still feels alive is Mina’s competence, and the cost of excluding her from the hunt that depends on her mind.

Van Helsing can sound, to a contemporary ear, like a trunk of proverbs with a crucifix in it. Stay with him. His role is to force the modern characters to admit that their categories are incomplete. Blood transfusion, in these chapters, is both a medical procedure and a ritual, and Stoker seems to know it is both.

## Reading the file

Try not to hunt only for the famous scenes: the mirror, the wolves, the nocturnal visits. Read the cutting-in of voices. Notice who is believed, who keeps the minutes, and how a group of people builds a chronology strong enough to act on. *Dracula* is a gothic novel with the soul of a case file. The monster is old. The method of fighting him is a stack of papers, passed from hand to hand, read aloud in a room before morning.
