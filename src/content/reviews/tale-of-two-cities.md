“It was the best of times, it was the worst of times.” Charles Dickens begins *A Tale of Two Cities* with a paragraph that can be recited by people who have not read the book. The paragraph earns its fame. It is a machine for holding contradiction, and the novel that follows is contradiction practiced at the scale of two capitals. London muddles along. Paris ignites. A doctor, destroyed by a letter he was imprisoned for, is **recalled to life**. A young woman raised in exile learns she has a father. A man who has wasted his talents decides, very late, to spend them.

This is Dickens in a tighter cut than the doorstop comedies. There are fewer wandering subplots, and the symbols repeat until they sound like a bell: footsteps, wine, knitting, a grave, a cart. The repetition is the design. History, in this book, is what happens when private injuries are copied onto a crowd.

## Wine on the street, names in a register

The scene of the broken wine cask in Saint Antoine is almost too clear, and it works anyway. Poor people kneel to drink what has spilled. A man writes “BLOOD” on a wall with the dregs. Dickens is warning you, early, that hunger will be given a symbol and the symbol will be acted out. Madame Defarge’s knitting is the same idea in a quieter register. She sits at the wine shop and records the names of the condemned in stitches. It is domestic craft turned into a future execution list. You do not forget her, and you are not meant to rest in a simple view of her. The revolution Dickens shows has reasons. It also eats the reasons.

Dr. Manette’s imprisonment, before the novel properly starts, is the seed. He is buried alive by a noble family’s privilege, and the noble family’s younger generation will inherit a debt it did not personally sign. Dickens is interested in that inheritance. Sydney Carton and Charles Darnay look alike, which is the plot’s hinge and the moral’s hinge. One man is associated with a guilty class and tries to detach himself. The other has no title and has spent years detaching himself from his own promise. The resemblance lets Dickens ask what a life is worth when it can be substituted.

> “I see a beautiful city and a brilliant people rising from this abyss.”

## The ending everyone knows, and the middle they skip

Carton’s last act is so famous it can eclipse the book’s better question: what do you do with a self you have misspent, short of a scaffold? The middle chapters in London, with Jarvis Lorry’s loyalty and Miss Pross’s stubborn love, are the counterweight to Paris. They are not as theatrical. They are the case for ordinary fidelity. If you read only for the guillotine, you will think Dickens believes in grand redemption scenes. He does. He also believes in a banker who keeps showing up, and a woman who will face down a villain in a hallway because her friend is in the carriage.

## A reading note

Keep the two families straight, and do not be ashamed of a pencil. The book’s coincidences are real; Dickens liked a design you can see. Under the design is a hard thought about political violence. He grants the oppressed their fury and then refuses to sanctify a machine that no longer distinguishes. The paired sentences of the opening are a promise the last page keeps: a time can be two things at once, and a single life can still choose which sentence it will finish.

:::spoiler
Sydney Carton takes Charles Darnay’s place at the guillotine, buying the other man’s life with a face similar enough to pass. The famous last lines are his, imagined rather than spoken, walking toward a death that finally spends the talents he had wasted. Dickens asks you to find that sacrifice moving, and he builds the whole second half of the book so that you might.
:::
