Raskolnikov’s room is so small he can lie on the bed and touch the door. From that cupboard of a life he builds a theory large enough to justify a killing. Fyodor Dostoevsky’s *Crime and Punishment* is the story of what a theory does when it has to walk down an actual staircase, past a caretaker, into a flat where a woman is still alive and inconvenient.

The plot can be told in a sentence, and the sentence is not the experience. A broke former student in St. Petersburg murders a pawnbroker, and also her sister, who comes home at the wrong moment. He spends the rest of the novel trying to live in the same city as the act. Dostoevsky is less interested in whether we can solve the case than in whether a person can survive his own reasons.

## Ideas with a temperature

Raskolnikov has an article in his past about “extraordinary” men who may step over ordinary laws for the sake of a greater good. Napoleon is the example he cannot stop handling. The article sounds, in isolation, like a dormitory argument. Dostoevsky puts it in a body that is hungry, proud, sick, and capable of sudden generosity. Raskolnikov gives his last money to a stranger’s family and then despises himself for the softness. He rehearses the murder like a philosopher and botches it like a terrified man. The gap between the two is the novel.

Petersburg does as much work as any speech. The heat, the dust, the taverns, the tiny rooms stacked on narrow landings: the city feels like a fever that has learned architecture. People talk too much because there is nowhere to put a secret. Porfiry Petrovich, the investigator, understands this. He does not need a brilliant trap. He needs time, and a suspect who cannot stop thinking in his presence.

> “Pain and suffering are always inevitable for a large intelligence and a deep heart.”

## The people the theory forgot

The book keeps a count the theory refuses to keep:

1. The pawnbroker, who was the argument’s chosen target.
2. **Lizaveta**, who was only in the doorway.
3. A household — Sonya, Dunya, a mother — asked to live inside the aftermath.

Lizaveta, the pawnbroker’s sister, is the fact that will not fit the essay. She was not the “louse” in the argument. She was a person in the doorway. Dostoevsky keeps returning, by other means, to people a grand idea would step over: Sonya, who has kept her family alive by means the respectable world condemns; her father, who drinks the shame and the love together; Dunya, Raskolnikov’s sister, who is being bargained toward a marriage that looks practical from a distance and looks like a sale up close.

Sonya is sometimes described as a symbol of redemption. She is more interesting as a character who has already been forced to do moral arithmetic without the luxury of theory. When she reads the raising of Lazarus aloud, the scene is intimate and almost unbearably earnest. You do not have to share the novel’s Christianity to feel what it is doing: placing a man who wanted to be extraordinary in front of a woman who cannot afford that fantasy.

## Reading without a lecture

The book is long, hot, and full of conversations that spiral. That spiral is the method. Dostoevsky does not stand above his characters with a clean moral. He lets them argue past the point of dignity, then sends them back into the street. If you read only for the crime, the punishment will feel like an epilogue. If you read for the way an idea infects a household, the Siberian ending is simply the first time Raskolnikov stops trying to win the argument and starts trying to live beside another person.

:::spoiler
Raskolnikov confesses, not in a triumphant debate but in a police office after a long unraveling, and is sentenced to Siberia. Sonya follows him. The epilogue, which can feel like a different book, is the first stretch of time in which he stops defending the theory and begins, slowly, to accept a life that includes another person. Dostoevsky does not pretend the conversion is complete. He only shows it starting.
:::
